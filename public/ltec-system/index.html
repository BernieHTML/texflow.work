How it works

LTEC is an Organic Rankine Cycle heat engine, modelled on OTEC — the ocean systems that make power from the difference between warm surface water and the cold deep. Here the two reservoirs are a permanently shadowed crater floor, which has not seen sunlight in billions of years and sits near 28 K, and the high-illumination ground near the Shackleton rim, which the Sun grazes almost continuously and which the paper models at 118 K.

A working fluid is pumped through pipes in the sunlit region, where it evaporates. The vapour spins a turbine and its generator, then returns to a condenser in the shadow, where it gives up its remaining heat and is pumped back out to the rim.

The four equations

ηC = 1 − Tc / Th ← Carnot efficiency
QH = A × S × α × f ← heat collected, kW
Wmax = ηC × QH ← theoretical maximum
Wprac = ηORC × ηC × QH ← with ηORC = 0.44

A is the projected pipe area, diameter × length, exactly as the paper takes it: a 0.1 m pipe running 100 m counts as 10 m². Nothing is rounded between those four steps; only what you see is rounded, to three significant figures.

On the 2,575 kW in the paper

At 2,500 m² this page shows 2,595 kW theoretical maximum where the paper prints 2,575 kW. The difference is rounding, not physics: the paper rounds the Carnot efficiency to 0.76 and the per-10 m² output to 10.3 kW before scaling up.

What this is not

A first-order, idealised, upper-bound model, built for illustration during a talk. It is not an engineering design: it assumes perfect heat transfer, and says nothing about working-fluid selection, pumping power, pressure drop, pipe mass, or how ten kilometres of plumbing reaches the lunar south pole. Treat every number as the best case physics permits.

Reality check

The paper models collection only. A real pipe at 118 K also radiates heat back into space from its whole circumference, and whatever the turbine does not convert has to be dumped somewhere. Turn this on to subtract both — every number above updates too.
